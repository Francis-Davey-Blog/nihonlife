In Japan, when you have been away &mdash; it doesn't matter whether a business trip, or holiday or for some other reason &mdash; what you are *supposed* to do is bring back bring back small gifts of food such as cakes or biscuits to share with your colleagues, friends, relatives, neighbours or others you feel you owe something to. 

These gifts are known as "Omiyage" (お土産). I have seen it suggested that the custom dates back to the restrictions on travel imposed by the Tokugawa Shogunate during their period of rule known as the Edo Jidai (conventionally 1603 - 1868). In order to prevent rival samurai families from forming alliances, anyone travelling between domains would need a pass issued by the authorities along with a good reason for the journey. The shoguns were particularly keen that women did not travel between domains without authorisation in order to prevent unsanctioned marriage alliances between families.

But another cunning feature of Tokugawa rule meant that, despite all these controls, samurai were engaged in a considerable amount of travel. Most domain rulers (known as "daimyo") had to alternate between spending the year in Edo (now Tokyo) and their home domain and to take a "sufficient" number of retainers, a number that might be in the thousands for an important lord.The result was lots of samurai moving backward and forward over the main roads of Japan.

From the Tokugawa point of view this system, known as sankin-koutai ("alternate attendance") had great benefits. Every daimyo had to maintain appropriately sumptious accommodation in Edo, along with staff, even while not in attendance. Whilst in Edo, the daimyo and their samurai would need to be entertained with food, drink, theatre, music and other things I am sure you can imagine. All that money spent in Edo, turned what had been a fairly modest seaside town into one of the largest cities in the World. It also drained the daimyo of cash which might otherwise have been spent on opposing the Tokugawa.

Where does Omiyage come into this? The theory goes that this was a way for those unable to leave their domains to have a "taste" of the Japan they could not visit &mdash; like postcards, only edible. Thus an important part of Omiyage today is that it should somehow represent the produce of the area from which it comes. There are thus Omiyage shops in most tourist destinations as well as airports and stations. I have spent much time searching for just the right regional speciality to take back with me.

Just over a week ago, I returned from a brief tour of the nearer part of Kyushu, which is an area that has a number of active volcanoes and great deal more places where, driven by the hot rock under the surface, ot water comes out of the ground. In most places, the hot water comes out as a natural hot spring, or is deliberately piped or pumped out. Those hot springs were the main goal of our holiday, , mainly spent visiting hot springs.
